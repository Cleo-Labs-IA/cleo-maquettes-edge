A federal rule with no custom-made exemption, five years in

The Corded Window Coverings Regulations, made under the Canada Consumer Product Safety Act, replaced an earlier 2016 rule and entered into force on 1 May 2021. They set physical limits designed to stop a child pulling a cord into a loop around their neck: a reachable cord with one free end cannot exceed 22 cm, two cords that can connect end to end cannot form a resulting loop longer than 44 cm in perimeter, accessible small parts must not detach under a 90 N pulling force, and external components cannot contain more than 90 mg/kg of lead. Health Canada spent the first year after the rule took effect, from 1 May 2021 to 30 April 2022, on awareness and outreach rather than enforcement. Active compliance monitoring began on 1 May 2022, and the regulation applies to the manufacture and sale of every corded window covering sold in Canada, custom-made products included: there is no carve-out for a made-to-order roller shade cut to a customer's window, sold by a local workroom rather than a national retailer.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

The failure rate flipped from mostly-imported to mostly-domestic

In the fiscal year right after enforcement began, 29 of the 38 products Health Canada tested and failed were imports. A year later, only 12 of the 44 tested products were imports, meaning most of that round's failures were domestically produced.

02

'Custom-made' describes the sales channel, not a legal exemption

Health Canada's December 2023 to January 2024 recall batch covered 13 notices, 12 of them for Canadian-made products manufactured entirely after the rule existed, since 2021. A bespoke, made-to-order product carries the same obligation as a mass-market import.

03

Awareness has not caught up five years later

Health Canada's own testing rounds and recall notices are still finding new noncompliant window coverings on the market five years after the rule took effect and more than four years after active enforcement began.

1 May 2021

The Corded Window Coverings Regulations (SOR/2019-97) take effect, replacing a 2016 rule and setting cord-length, loop-size, small-parts and lead limits for every corded window covering sold in Canada.

1 May 2022

Health Canada's one-year awareness period ends; active compliance monitoring and enforcement begin. Within four months, at least 11 recall notices cover more than 350 noncompliant products.

FY 2022-2023

Health Canada lab-tests 38 window covering products: all 38 fail at least one requirement, 29 of them imports. The round leads to 16 recalls, a stop-sale order, 8 stop-distribution orders and 5 trader commitments.

FY 2023-2024

A second round tests 44 products: 43 fail, only 1 passes fully, and only 12 of the 44 samples are imports, meaning most of that round's failures were domestically produced.

19 Aug 2026

Health Canada recalls a custom-made Greystone Blinds roller shade for failing the same regulation: strangulation and choking hazard, no injuries reported in Canada.

The numbers behind 19 August

One number is the rule's own physical test: how large a loop a cord is allowed to form before it is a strangulation risk. Two are from Health Canada's own lab testing program, one year apart, showing how the split between imported and domestic failures moved.

44 cm

the maximum perimeter a loop formed by two connectable cords can reach under the regulation; a free-end cord alone is capped at 22 cm, both measured after a 35 N pull

29 of 38

the share of failed products that were imports in Health Canada's first full testing round after enforcement began: all 38 products tested failed at least one requirement

12 of 44

the share that were imports in the following year's round, where 43 of 44 products failed, meaning most of that round's noncompliant products were made domestically

The real subject: classification runs on a physical feature, not on origin or channel

Product-safety coverage defaults to an import story: cheap, high-volume goods arriving from overseas without local testing, a narrative border-enforcement statistics usually confirm. Health Canada's own compliance data on corded window coverings does not fit that story. The Corded Window Coverings Regulations trigger on one measurable attribute, whether an accessible cord can form a loop or free length beyond a fixed size, and that attribute is identical whether the product ships from an overseas factory or is cut and assembled the same week it is sold, in a workroom a few streets from the buyer's home. A custom shop selling direct to a homeowner is not a lower-risk channel under this rule: it is, on the regulator's most recent sampling, the more common source of noncompliance. That is a classification problem before it is an import-enforcement problem. A brand or manufacturer that only checks whether a product crossed a border to decide whether it needs a compliance file is applying the wrong filter: the rule is triggered by what the product is, a corded window covering with a specific measurable feature, not by where it came from.

Why it matters for brands

For any retail or home-goods brand selling window coverings, drapery hardware or other corded textile products into Canada, whether stocked, private-label or made-to-order, three things follow from 19 August. First, a made-to-order or small-batch production line is not a lower compliance priority: Health Canada's own testing shows domestically produced, custom-cut products failing at a rate at least as high as imports, and in its most recent round, higher. Second, the compliance trigger is a physical measurement, cord and loop length, small-parts retention force, lead content, not a sales channel or order type, so a per-SKU classification needs to check the product's actual cord and hardware design against the regulation's numeric limits, not assume a bespoke or direct-to-consumer line sits outside a rule written with mass retail in mind. Third, five years and multiple enforcement rounds have not closed the gap: a brand entering the Canadian market today, or auditing an existing made-to-order catalogue, is not checking a formality but a category where the regulator's own most recent sample found a 97.7% failure rate.

Two ways to read 19 August

The narrow read

Health Canada recalled one custom-made roller shade for a strangulation and choking hazard, with no injuries reported.

The structural read

Five years after this rule took effect, the regulator's own testing shows domestic, custom-made products failing it as often or more often than imports, evidence that the compliance gap sits in who assumes the rule does not apply to them, not in which border a product crossed.

Frequently asked questions

What did Health Canada recall on 19 August 2026?
A custom-made 3-inch black fascia manual roller shade manufactured by Greystone Blinds, recalled because it does not meet the Corded Window Coverings Regulations (SOR/2019-97) and poses a strangulation and choking hazard to young children. No injuries were reported in Canada.
Do Canada's corded window covering rules apply to custom-made and made-to-order products?
Yes. The Corded Window Coverings Regulations apply to the manufacture and sale of every corded window covering sold in Canada, with no exemption for custom-made, made-to-order or locally produced products. Health Canada's own compliance testing shows domestically made products failing the rule as often, or more often, than imports.
What do Canada's cord and loop limits actually require?
A reachable cord with one free end cannot exceed 22 cm, and two cords able to connect end to end cannot form a resulting loop longer than 44 cm in perimeter, both measured after a 35 N pull. Accessible small parts must not detach under a 90 N force, and external components cannot contain more than 90 mg/kg of lead. The rule took effect on 1 May 2021, with active enforcement beginning 1 May 2022.

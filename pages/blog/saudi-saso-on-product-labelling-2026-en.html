SASO has circulated a new on-product labelling requirement to users of its SABER import platform, covering ten technical regulations: LPG tankers, watercraft, tanks, trailers and semi-trailers, self-balancing electric boards, simple pressure vessels, child restraint systems and strollers, hydraulic connections and related products, solar photovoltaic systems, and building lifts. What actually changes on 1 October

SABER, Saudi Arabia's mandatory electronic platform for product registration and Certificates of Conformity, already requires an importer or local manufacturer to declare its identity as part of a shipment's technical file before a Certificate of Conformity can be issued. That declaration lived in the platform's data, not on the goods. The new circular closes that gap for ten specific technical regulations by requiring the same two data points, supplier name and commercial registration number, to be physically marked on the product itself: stamped, printed, engraved or otherwise permanently affixed, depending on what the product and its technical regulation allow. SASO frames the change as a verification measure rather than a new disclosure obligation in principle, since the supplier's identity is already collected on SABER for every certified product; what changes is that an inspector, a customs officer or a market-surveillance check can now confirm that identity by looking at the unit in front of them, rather than by pulling the SABER record for its declared certificate number. Conformity assessment bodies are directed to check for the mark before recommending a Certificate of Conformity, and SASO says it will itself run random sampling and technical-file reviews through SABER to confirm that check is actually happening.

Three details behind the 1 October deadline

01

On the product, not the box

The circular's requirement is for a mark on the product itself, separate from packaging labels and separate from the SABER dossier that already records supplier identity.

02

Two data points, ten technical regulations

Supplier name and commercial registration number are the only two fields required, but they apply across categories as different as strollers, solar panels and pressure vessels.

03

No mark, no certificate

SASO ties the requirement directly to Certificate of Conformity issuance on SABER: from 1 October, a missing mark blocks the certificate, not just the shipment.

2019

SABER goes live as the mandatory Saudi platform for product registration and Certificate of Conformity issuance, centralising supplier and product data for regulated imports.

Sep 2026

SASO circulates the on-product labelling requirement to SABER platform users, covering ten technical regulations and setting the physical-marking rule alongside the existing digital declaration.

1 Oct 2026

The requirement enters into force: SASO stops issuing Certificates of Conformity, for the ten covered technical regulations, to products that do not carry the supplier name and commercial registration number on the product itself.

The numbers behind the rule

One number is how many technical regulations the mark now applies to at once. One is how few data points the mark actually requires. The third is the calendar gap brands are working against, since the circular reached wide compliance-industry attention only weeks before it takes effect.

10 regulations

the technical regulations now requiring an on-product supplier mark, spanning LPG tankers, watercraft, tanks, trailers, self-balancing boards, pressure vessels, child restraint systems and strollers, hydraulic connections, solar photovoltaic systems and building lifts

2 data points

what the mark itself must contain: the supplier's name and its commercial registration number, nothing more, but placed on the unit rather than the packaging or the SABER file alone

1 Oct 2026

the date SASO stops issuing a Certificate of Conformity, for any of the ten covered technical regulations, to a product without the mark

The real subject: a data field is moving from the file to the unit

Most coverage of this circular treats it as a compliance-paperwork update: SABER now also wants the supplier's name and commercial registration number, add it to the checklist. That framing misses what makes the requirement operationally different from every other field SABER already collects. A declared supplier name in a digital dossier can be updated the moment a business detail changes: a new distributor, a corrected registration number, a renamed importer. A mark engraved into a pressure vessel, moulded into a stroller frame or printed onto a solar panel's junction box cannot be updated after the fact without physically reworking the unit, and for several of the ten covered categories, LPG tankers, watercraft, tanks, lifts, that marking has to survive the product's working environment for years. SASO's own enforcement mechanism makes the stakes concrete: the mark is not a labelling nicety checked once at customs, it is now a precondition SASO checks against SABER data before it will issue the Certificate of Conformity a shipment needs to clear at all, and SASO says it will keep sampling products after the fact to confirm the mark that conformity assessment bodies signed off on is actually there. A supplier that treats this as a SABER data-entry task, rather than as a change to the physical product and its tooling, is the one most likely to have a compliant-looking digital file and a shipment that still fails inspection.

Why it matters for brands

Two groups of Cleo's usual clients sit directly inside this circular's scope, even though most read it as an industrial-equipment rule. First, any brand selling child restraint systems or strollers into Saudi Arabia, a consumer category with the same safety sensitivity as toys or juvenile products anywhere else, now needs its supplier name and commercial registration number physically marked on every unit, not only declared on SABER, before a new Certificate of Conformity will issue after 1 October: a stroller frame moulded or printed before that requirement was on a supplier's radar is a retooling problem, not a paperwork fix, and retooling on a hard deadline is exactly the kind of change that needs weeks of lead time a mid-September circular does not leave. Second, any brand or distributor supplying residential or small-scale solar photovoltaic kits, an increasingly common consumer and prosumer product, faces the identical mark requirement on the unit itself. Third, and more generally, any compliance or product-data team managing SKUs certified through SABER should treat this as a signal about where Saudi enforcement is heading: SASO is closing the gap between what a supplier declares digitally and what is physically verifiable on the product, and a data model that only tracks 'SABER certificate: on file' without a linked, per-SKU record of what mark actually ships on the unit will not catch a mismatch until an inspector does.

Two ways to read the 1 October deadline

The narrow read

From 1 October, Saudi Arabia adds a supplier-identity field to the label of products under ten technical regulations, verified through the existing SABER platform.

The structural read

SASO is moving a data field that used to live only in a digital dossier onto the physical product, and tying its absence directly to Certificate of Conformity issuance. For categories like child restraint systems and solar panels, that turns a database update into a tooling and lead-time problem, on a deadline set weeks, not months, before it bites.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly changes in Saudi Arabia on 1 October 2026?
SASO's on-product labelling requirement enters into force for products covered by ten technical regulations, including LPG tankers, watercraft, tanks, trailers and semi-trailers, self-balancing electric boards, simple pressure vessels, child restraint systems and strollers, hydraulic connections and related products, solar photovoltaic systems, and building lifts. From that date, the supplier's name (importer or local manufacturer) and its commercial registration number must be marked directly on the product itself, and SASO will not issue a Certificate of Conformity through the SABER platform for a covered product that lacks the mark.
Is a supplier name printed on the packaging or filed in SABER enough to comply?
No. The circular requires the mark on the product itself, in addition to whatever supplier information already appears on packaging or in the SABER technical file. SASO's stated verification method, random sampling and technical-file review through SABER, is built to catch exactly the gap between a compliant-looking digital declaration and a physical unit that does not carry the mark.
Does this requirement reach consumer products like strollers and solar kits, or only industrial equipment?
It reaches both. Most of the ten covered technical regulations are industrial or heavy-equipment categories, LPG tankers, watercraft, tanks, pressure vessels, building lifts, but child restraint systems and strollers, and solar photovoltaic systems including smaller residential kits, are consumer-facing categories inside the same scope and the same 1 October deadline.

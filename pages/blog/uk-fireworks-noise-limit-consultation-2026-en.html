On 16 July 2026, the UK's Office for Product Safety and Standards opened a 12-week consultation proposing to cut the legal noise limit for consumer fireworks from 120 to 110 A-weighted impulse decibels, a change OPSS estimates would remove around a third of today's consumer firework range from general sale. The consultation, open until 7 October 2026, also asks whether more products should join the existing banned list and whether Christmas crackers should lose their age restriction. The noise limit is a reserved, UK-wide product standard, but it would apply on top of, not instead of, Scotland's own licence, purchase-hour and Firework Control Zone rules, which already govern who can buy and use a firework regardless of how quiet it is.

What OPSS actually proposed on 16 July

The consultation, published by OPSS on 16 July 2026 and running until 11:59pm on 7 October 2026, sets out three separate proposals in one document. The first, and the one carrying the concrete number, would lower the maximum permitted noise level for consumer fireworks (BS EN 15947 category F2, the class sold to the general public for back-garden use) from 120 to 110 dB(A,imp), a peak sound-pressure reading with A-weighting and impulse time-weighting, taken 1 metre above ground at 8 metres from the firing point. OPSS states the ten-decibel cut would roughly halve how loud the noisiest products currently on sale are perceived to be, and estimates it would move around a third of today's consumer firework range out of general sale entirely, into a category restricted to licensed professional users. The second proposal asks whether more individual products should be added to the banned list already maintained under the Pyrotechnic Articles (Safety) Regulations 2015 (PAR 2015), the instrument, in force since 17 August 2015, that implements the EU's Pyrotechnic Articles Directive 2013/29/EU and its traceability directive 2014/58/EU in UK law. The third reviews the regulatory treatment of products currently classed as lower-risk, including a proposal to drop the existing age restriction on buying Christmas crackers, on the grounds that their risk profile does not match the fireworks category they are currently regulated alongside. OPSS grounds the noise proposal in its own research from December 2025: among respondents who said they disliked firework noise, 91% cited its effect on animals, 71% cited its effect on the peace and quiet of their community, and 68% cited its effect on vulnerable people. The government frames the package as part of its Safer Streets Mission against anti-social behaviour, and the Department for Business and Trade says it will analyse consultation responses, including with AI-assisted tools, once the window closes in October.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

A 12-week consultation, not a rule

OPSS is asking for views until 7 October 2026. Nothing in the noise limit, the banned-list additions or the cracker exemption has legal effect yet; the department still has to review responses, decide a final position, and legislate.

02

The number that moves sits in a referenced standard, not the regulation itself

The current 120 dB ceiling for category F2 fireworks isn't written into the Pyrotechnic Articles (Safety) Regulations 2015 directly; it comes from BS EN 15947, the harmonised test standard the regulations rely on for conformity. A 110 dB proposal changes what that referenced standard requires, not the regulation's own text.

03

A UK-wide standard lands on top of a devolved sale regime, not instead of it

PAR 2015 and any noise-limit change to it are reserved, UK-wide product law. Scotland already requires its own fireworks licence to buy, possess or use most fireworks, under the Fireworks and Pyrotechnic Articles (Scotland) Act 2022. Passing the new noise test would not exempt a product from that separate licence.

The real subject: a reserved test does not erase a devolved sale-and-use patchwork

Most coverage of the 16 July consultation ran with the noise number, a single UK-wide decibel figure that reads as a tidy, uniform fix. Product safety standards under PAR 2015 are a reserved matter, set from Westminster and applying across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland alike, which is exactly why OPSS is consulting all four nations on the change. But whether a specific firework can be sold to, bought by, or set off by a member of the public was never governed by PAR 2015 alone, and the consultation does not propose to change that. Scotland already layers its own rules on top: the Fireworks and Pyrotechnic Articles (Scotland) Act 2022 requires a licence to buy, possess or use most fireworks; the Fireworks (Scotland) Miscellaneous Amendments Regulations 2021 restrict purchase to a fixed daily window and cap a single transaction at 5kg of net explosive mass; and since 22 June 2023, local councils can designate Firework Control Zones where igniting a firework at all, even on private property, is a criminal offence carrying up to a £5,000 fine or six months in prison. Northern Ireland runs its own separate framework under the Explosives (Fireworks) Regulations (Northern Ireland) 2002. None of those instruments care what a product's dB(A,imp) reading is. A firework that clears the proposed 110 dB threshold, and stays classified for general retail everywhere else in the UK, can still be unsellable to an unlicensed Scottish retailer, unbuyable outside the legal purchase window, and unlawful to ignite inside a council's Control Zone. The consultation tightens one input into a much longer compliance chain; it does not shorten the chain.

Why it matters for brands

Any retailer running a seasonal fireworks range, garden centres, DIY chains, supermarkets stocking Bonfire Night or New Year lines, party and celebration-goods brands, needs two separate layers of product data to act on this consultation, not one. The first is technical: a per-SKU BS EN 15947 dB(A,imp) test result, so a brand knows before October which lines in its current catalogue would flip from general retail (category F2) to professional-only if the 110 dB proposal is adopted, rather than finding out when a shipment is refused or a listing is pulled. The second layer is jurisdictional, and it does not move regardless of the first: which SKUs require a licensed Scottish seller, which fall inside the 5kg-per-transaction and fixed-hours purchase rules, and which risk landing inside a local authority's Firework Control Zone once one is designated. A brand that only tracks the noise threshold will correctly predict which products survive general sale in England and Wales, and will still get the Scottish and Northern Irish answer wrong, because that answer was never a function of decibels. Product data that maps each SKU to both the technical standard and the nation-specific sale-and-use rule, updated as this consultation moves toward a final position, is what lets a brand answer “can I sell this, here, to this buyer” as one question instead of guessing at it twice.

Two ways to read 16 July

The narrow read

The UK proposed a quieter fireworks rule after years of noise complaints: a routine product-safety tightening, unremarkable on its own.

The structural read

A reserved, UK-wide noise standard would land on top of a devolved sale-and-use regime that already treats the same SKU differently by nation, and passing the new dB test would not touch a single one of the licence, purchase-window or Control Zone rules that actually decide whether that SKU reaches a shelf, or can legally be lit, in Scotland.

Frequently asked questions

What did OPSS actually propose on 16 July 2026, and is it already law?
No. The UK's Office for Product Safety and Standards opened a 12-week consultation, "Fireworks and pyrotechnics in the UK", open for responses until 7 October 2026. Its central proposal would cut the legal noise ceiling for consumer (category F2) fireworks from 120 to 110 A-weighted impulse decibels, measured under the harmonised standard BS EN 15947. It also consults on adding more products to the existing banned list under the Pyrotechnic Articles (Safety) Regulations 2015 and on removing the age restriction on Christmas crackers. Nothing in the proposal has legal effect yet: the Department for Business and Trade must review responses and decide a final position before any change is made.
How many consumer fireworks would actually be affected if the 110 dB limit is adopted?
OPSS estimates that around a third of the UK's current consumer firework range would fail the proposed 110 dB(A,imp) test and would be reclassified out of general retail sale, restricted instead to licensed professional users. The department also estimates the ten-decibel cut would roughly halve how loud the noisiest products on sale today are perceived to be. Those figures come from the consultation document itself and are proposals, not finalised reclassifications.
If a firework passes the new UK noise limit, can it automatically be sold in Scotland?
No. The noise limit under the Pyrotechnic Articles (Safety) Regulations 2015 is a reserved, UK-wide product-safety standard, but Scotland separately requires a licence to buy, possess or use most fireworks under the Fireworks and Pyrotechnic Articles (Scotland) Act 2022, caps a single purchase at 5kg of net explosive mass and restricts buying hours under the Fireworks (Scotland) Miscellaneous Amendments Regulations 2021, and, since 22 June 2023, lets local councils designate Firework Control Zones where igniting a firework at all is a criminal offence. None of those rules depend on a product's decibel rating, so clearing the new noise test does not clear any of Scotland's separate sale-and-use requirements.

On 25 September, the US recalled 4,596 pounds of ready-to-eat chicken stew imported from South Korea: no contamination was found, only a shipment that never cleared the port-of-entry check every meat and poultry import must pass

On 25 September 2026, FSIS announced that Sempio Food Services Inc. is recalling about 4,596 pounds of ready-to-eat chicken stew, 31.75-oz (900 g) pouches of CLASSIC and RICH varieties produced 30 March 2026 in South Korea under establishment number GIA15001, because the shipment was imported into the US without the benefit of import reinspection, the check FSIS runs on every meat and poultry import before it may enter US commerce. FSIS found the gap through routine retail surveillance, not a contamination finding, and has logged no confirmed reports of illness or injury. By then the product had already reached distributors and retailers in eight states. The recall is a reminder that the port-of-entry check meant to catch this kind of gap does not always catch it there: a downstream retail check did instead, after the product was already on shelves.

What FSIS actually found, and what it did not find

The recall covers two chicken stew varieties sold in cardboard boxes containing 31.75-oz (900 g) pouches, produced on 30 March 2026 in South Korea under establishment number GIA15001 and carrying the shipping mark MK-2026-0422-01. The recall notice states the reason plainly: the shipment was imported without the benefit of import reinspection, the document-and-physical check FSIS runs on every meat and poultry shipment before it may enter US commerce. FSIS says it found the gap during routine surveillance activities, and separately confirms it has no reports of illness or injury connected to the product. Those two facts sit side by side in the same notice: a full recall, and no safety finding to justify it beyond the missing check itself.

Import reinspection is not a single stamp; it is a layered check FSIS runs on every shipment of meat, poultry and egg products entering the country. Inspectors first review the foreign certificate accompanying the shipment, then examine the product itself for condition and correct labeling, and log the result in the Public Health Information System, the database FSIS uses to track reinspection outcomes by country and by exporting establishment. Product that passes is stamped with the USDA mark of inspection and enters US commerce as though it had been produced domestically; product that fails is marked "US Refused Entry" and must be exported, destroyed, or converted to animal food within 45 days. Neither the recall notice nor the coverage of it explains how this specific shipment moved past that layered check and reached retail shelves in eight states before FSIS caught the gap.

The real subject: the check that is supposed to stop this at the border caught it downstream instead

Most coverage of a food recall defaults to a hazard template: a product is pulled because something was found wrong with it. This one reads differently once the two stated facts are held together. FSIS's import reinspection system exists precisely to catch a shipment like this one before it ever reaches a distributor: document review, physical examination and a database record, all completed at the port of entry, before the product is allowed to enter commerce at all. That is where this gap should have been caught. Instead, it surfaced through routine retail surveillance, after the chicken stew had already reached distributors and retailers in eight states. The product itself was never shown to be unsafe; what failed was the compliance record that is supposed to prove, independently of the food's actual condition, that a shipment legally cleared the border. A recall built on that kind of gap says less about what is in the pouch than about whether the paperwork trail behind it was complete, and in this case, the system's own downstream check is what caught what its upstream check did not.

Why it matters for brands

Three groups should read past the food-safety framing. First, any brand or importer bringing meat, poultry or egg products into the US needs a record of the FSIS-specific reinspection outcome for each shipment, logged in the Public Health Information System, not just a customs clearance confirmation from a broker or freight forwarder. The two are separate systems tracking separate questions, and this recall shows a shipment can clear general customs processing while still lacking the FSIS-specific reinspection stamp that determines whether it may legally enter commerce. Second, because this gap was caught at retail rather than at the port of entry, brands should not treat the border check as an infallible backstop: a compliance program that assumes "it reached our warehouse, so it must have cleared reinspection" has no independent way to verify that assumption until a downstream surveillance check, or a recall notice, proves otherwise. Third, recall scope here tracks the shipment identified by its shipping mark and establishment number, not a hazard threshold: both SKUs tied to shipping mark MK-2026-0422-01 were recalled in full, across all eight states of distribution, even with zero illness reports, because the missing reinspection record applies to the whole shipment rather than to a subset of units. A brand's own compliance tracking needs shipment-level import status recorded alongside product-quality data, since a demonstrably clean product can still trigger a full recall on the paperwork alone.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly did FSIS recall, and why?
On 25 September 2026, FSIS announced that Sempio Food Services Inc. is recalling about 4,596 pounds of ready-to-eat chicken stew, sold as 31.75-oz (900 g) pouches under the CLASSIC and RICH names, produced 30 March 2026 in South Korea under establishment number GIA15001 and shipping mark MK-2026-0422-01. The stated reason is that the shipment was imported into the US without the benefit of import reinspection, the check FSIS requires before any meat or poultry shipment may enter US commerce. The product had already reached distributors and retailers in Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania and Virginia.
Does "imported without the benefit of import reinspection" mean the chicken stew was contaminated?
No. FSIS states it has no confirmed reports of illness or injury tied to the product, and found the gap through routine retail surveillance rather than any contamination finding. The recall's basis is that the required import reinspection record for this shipment is missing, a compliance and paperwork failure distinct from a finding that the food itself is unsafe.
What is FSIS import reinspection, and why does this matter for brands selling meat or poultry products into the US?
FSIS reinspects every shipment of meat, poultry and egg products entering the US: reviewing the foreign certificate, physically examining the product and labeling, and logging the result in its Public Health Information System before the product may enter commerce. This is separate from general customs clearance handled by a broker or freight forwarder. This recall shows that gap can go undetected at the border and only surface later, through downstream retail surveillance, which means brands and importers need their own record of the FSIS-specific reinspection outcome for each shipment, not just a customs clearance confirmation.

What the alert actually says, and what it leans on

PSA9 is narrow and specific. It covers baby sleeping bags marketed or intended for a baby to sleep in, unsupervised, in a cot or crib, that either have no armholes or include a hood. OPSS's stated reasoning: without armholes to hold a baby in position, a child can slip down inside the bag, which can cause overheating and suffocation; a hood can cover a baby's nose and mouth and cause the same harm. OPSS also links overheating to a higher risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). The alert explicitly carves out two categories that look similar but are not in scope: products worn in a pram, such as pramsuits or footmuffs, and products designed specifically to swaddle a baby. It also states that it does not apply to sleeping bags that meet BS EN 16781:2018, the voluntary British Standard for children's sleep bags, which already requires armholes and prohibits hoods on this product type.

That reference point is where the alert's timing gets interesting. BS EN 16781:2018 is the UK implementation of a European standard, EN 16781, first published in 2018. On 3 September 2026, CEN published EN 16781:2026, a revision of the same standard, restructured to a hazard-based approach. Among the changes: a child height range and sleep bag length were added to the requirement table; requirements for slide fasteners, buttons and press fasteners were clarified, referencing the CEN/TS 17394 series; a new requirement for weighted sleep bags was added, a product type the 2018 edition did not address at all; entrapment-hazard requirements for textile and non-textile materials were clarified; thermal-resistance testing was detailed with a reference to EN 17667; and new defined terms were added, including traceability. None of the official or standards-body sources reviewed for this article give a confirmed UK withdrawal date for BS EN 16781:2018, so the two versions currently coexist with no announced cutover.

The real subject: a compliance certificate names a standard, and standards have version numbers

Most coverage of PSA9 reads it as a simple rule: buy sleeping bags with armholes, not hoods. That rule is correct, but it is not the whole compliance picture. The alert's safe harbor is a named, dated document, BS EN 16781:2018, not a general principle. A brand holding a test report against that exact edition can show a product is exempt from PSA9 today. The same test report says nothing about EN 16781:2026, the CEN revision published four weeks earlier, which adds rules the 2018 edition never contained: a requirement for weighted sleep bags, a defined child height and length range, clarified fastener rules referencing a separate technical specification series, and a new definition of traceability. A sleeping bag can clear the bar PSA9 is actually enforcing, armholes present, no hood, BS EN 16781:2018 on file, while being silent on every requirement the 2026 revision introduced, because nobody has yet told the market when, or whether, UK retailers need to move to the new edition.

Why it matters for brands

Three groups selling baby sleeping bags into the UK, or into the EU under the parallel European standard, are affected in different ways PSA9's headline does not spell out. First, any brand whose current test certificates cite BS EN 16781:2018 should treat that document's exact edition date as a live product-compliance field, not a fixed fact, because the European reference it tracks has already moved; a supplier declaration that only says “compliant with EN 16781”, without the year, cannot show which set of requirements, armholes and hoods only, or the fuller 2026 scope, was actually tested. Second, brands selling weighted sleep bags, a category the 2018 edition never addressed at all, have no prior UK or EU baseline to certify against and should expect the 2026 requirements to become the reference once a transition date is set, rather than waiting for a recall to find out. Third, every brand in this category needs its product records to carry the standard version as structured data next to the test result itself, shell fabric, fasteners and thermal rating included, because a safety alert that exempts “BS EN 16781:2018” by name will not protect a product whose paperwork only says “tested to BS EN 16781”.

Two ways to read PSA9

The narrow read

From 1 October 2026, UK parents and carers are told not to buy or use baby sleeping bags for unsupervised cot sleep that lack armholes or include a hood, under OPSS Product Safety Alert PSA9.

The structural read

PSA9's safe harbor names one dated edition of a European standard that was revised four weeks earlier; a brand's compliance now depends on tracking which version number sits behind each test certificate, not only on whether a sleeping bag has armholes.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly did the UK tell parents to stop doing on 1 October 2026?
The Office for Product Safety and Standards issued Product Safety Alert PSA9, telling parents and carers not to buy, and to stop using, baby sleeping bags intended for unsupervised sleep in a cot or crib if the product lacks armholes or includes a hood, because of overheating and suffocation risk. It does not cover pramsuits, footmuffs, or products designed to swaddle a baby.
Does a sleeping bag tested against BS EN 16781:2018 automatically meet the newer EN 16781:2026?
Not necessarily. EN 16781:2026, published by CEN on 3 September 2026, restructured the standard around a hazard-based approach and added requirements the 2018 edition did not contain, including rules for weighted sleep bags, a defined child height and length range, and a new traceability definition. A 2018 test report does not demonstrate compliance with those added requirements, even though PSA9 currently exempts products meeting the 2018 edition.
Has the UK set a date to withdraw BS EN 16781:2018 in favor of the 2026 revision?
No confirmed UK withdrawal date for BS EN 16781:2018 appears in the official OPSS alert or in the standards-body sources reviewed for this article as of its publication date. The two editions currently coexist, with PSA9 referencing the 2018 text as its safe harbor.

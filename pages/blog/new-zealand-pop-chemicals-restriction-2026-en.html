On 9 October, New Zealand lists three chemicals as banned: for two of them the real import and manufacture ban doesn't start until December, and one comes with a carve-out for recycled PVC in sports surfaces

On 7 September 2026, New Zealand added chlorpyrifos, LC-PFCAs and MCCPs to its Hazardous Substances and New Organisms Act schedules under the Stockholm Convention. The Schedule 1AA listing takes effect 9 October 2026, but the operative import and manufacture ban sits in a separate schedule: it lands 16 December 2026 for LC-PFCAs and MCCPs, and 8 July 2027 for chlorpyrifos. What actually changes, and on which date

New Zealand controls hazardous chemicals through two linked but distinct schedules of the HSNO Act. Schedule 1AA is the country's own record of which substances it recognises as Persistent Organic Pollutants under the Stockholm Convention, the international treaty that lists chemicals for global phase-out; adding a substance here is a treaty-reporting step. Schedule 2A is the schedule that actually does the legal work: an entry there triggers the Act's prohibition on importing, manufacturing, using or storing that substance, unless the same entry carries a named exemption. The Order made on 7 September 2026 adds chlorpyrifos, LC-PFCAs and MCCPs to both schedules, but on different clocks. The Schedule 1AA listing, covering all three substances, takes effect on 9 October 2026. The Schedule 2A entries, the ones that make the substances illegal to import or manufacture, take effect later and not on the same date for all three: 16 December 2026 for LC-PFCAs and MCCPs, and 8 July 2027 for chlorpyrifos.

The same Order also updates the existing Schedule 2A entry for UV-328, a separate substance already restricted, to add exemptions agreed at the 12th Conference of the Parties to the Stockholm Convention in May 2025. UV-328 is not one of the three newly listed substances; the update simply keeps New Zealand's domestic schedule aligned with the international treaty text it implements. For MCCPs specifically, the Order writes in a narrower carve-out than a blanket ban would suggest: recycled PVC that contains MCCPs keeps a time-limited continued-use allowance when it goes into one of four named applications, traffic cones, roadside barriers, plastic sports courts and synthetic turf. No equivalent product-category exemption applies to LC-PFCAs or to chlorpyrifos.

Three details behind the 9 October date

01

One order, two different clocks

Schedule 1AA lists the three substances as POPs from 9 October 2026. Schedule 2A, the schedule that actually bans import and manufacture, runs on later, separate dates.

02

Two of the three real bans start in December, not October

The Schedule 2A entries for LC-PFCAs and MCCPs take effect 16 December 2026. Chlorpyrifos follows nine months later, on 8 July 2027.

03

A named carve-out inside the same ban

Recycled PVC containing MCCPs keeps a time-limited allowance in four named uses: traffic cones, roadside barriers, plastic sports courts and synthetic turf.

The real subject: a listing date reported as a ban date, and a ban that reads a product before it reads a substance

Most coverage of this Order compresses it into a single line: New Zealand bans three POP chemicals from 9 October 2026. That line conflates two schedules doing two different jobs. Schedule 1AA is New Zealand's bookkeeping against an international treaty; an entry there records that the country now recognises a substance as a Stockholm Convention POP, which is a necessary step but not, by itself, an operative control on anyone's supply chain. Schedule 2A is where the HSNO Act actually reaches into a warehouse or a purchase order: an entry there is what makes import, manufacture, use or storage illegal, and it is that schedule's own dates, not Schedule 1AA's, that a compliance calendar should track. For two of the three substances added by this Order, those dates fall ten weeks after the headline date everyone is citing; for the third, chlorpyrifos, they fall nine months after it. A brand that reformulates against 9 October has moved early, which is not a mistake, but a brand that assumes nothing is required until 9 October has read the wrong schedule. The MCCP entry adds a second, separate test on top of the calendar one: even after 16 December, the ban does not reach recycled PVC in four named uses. Answering whether a given SKU is covered is no longer just a date question; it is a material-and-end-use question that only a product's own bill of materials, not the regulation's headline, can settle.

Why it matters for brands

Three groups sell into or manufacture for New Zealand under this Order, and each is reading a different clock. First, brands using MCCPs as plasticisers or flame retardants in PVC-based goods, flooring, cabling, inflatable and outdoor gear, sports surfaces and synthetic turf, need their 16 December 2026 deadline confirmed against their own bill of materials, and, where the product is recycled PVC destined for one of the four named uses, need the exemption's conditions checked rather than assumed; a product that looks like a qualifying use on the shelf can still fail the exemption on its actual material composition. Second, brands using LC-PFCA-based water-repellent, stain-resistant or grease-proof finishes on apparel, footwear, leather goods and food-contact packaging have until 16 December 2026 to reformulate, not 9 October; treating the earlier date as the deadline risks a rushed, unnecessary changeover, while treating it as irrelevant risks missing that the real deadline is still ten weeks closer than the July 2027 date attached to the third substance. Third, any brand relying on natural-fibre inputs, cotton in particular, that may carry chlorpyrifos residues from agricultural treatment has a longer runway, to 8 July 2027, that most coverage does not separate from the October headline. In each case, the question a compliance team actually needs answered, which substance is in which SKU, at what concentration, in which material and end-use, is a product-data question the calendar alone cannot resolve.

Two ways to read the 9 October date

The narrow read

From 9 October 2026, New Zealand lists chlorpyrifos, LC-PFCAs and MCCPs as Persistent Organic Pollutants, prohibiting them under the Hazardous Substances and New Organisms Act.

The structural read

The October date only records the listing; the ban that reaches a purchase order lands ten weeks later for two of the three substances and nine months later for the third, and one of them keeps a named exemption that a product's own material and end-use, not the calendar, decides.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly happens in New Zealand on 9 October 2026?
Chlorpyrifos, LC-PFCAs and MCCPs are formally listed under Schedule 1AA of the Hazardous Substances and New Organisms Act, New Zealand's record of substances it recognises as Persistent Organic Pollutants under the Stockholm Convention. This is a treaty-reporting step; it does not, on its own, make importing, manufacturing or using these substances illegal. That prohibition comes from a separate schedule, Schedule 2A, whose entries for these substances take effect on later dates.
When does importing or manufacturing these substances actually become illegal?
The Schedule 2A entries for LC-PFCAs and MCCPs take effect on 16 December 2026, ten weeks after the 9 October listing date. The Schedule 2A entry for chlorpyrifos takes effect later still, on 8 July 2027. From each respective date, importing, manufacturing, using or storing that substance in New Zealand is prohibited unless a specific exemption in the schedule applies.
Does the MCCP ban cover every PVC product?
No. The Order carves out recycled PVC that contains MCCPs when it is used in one of four named applications: traffic cones, roadside barriers, plastic sports courts and synthetic turf. That exemption is time-limited and specific to those uses; it does not extend to virgin PVC or to other product categories, and no equivalent product-category exemption exists for LC-PFCAs or for chlorpyrifos.

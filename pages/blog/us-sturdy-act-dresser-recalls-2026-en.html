In the first half of September, CPSC recalled three more dresser lines for violating the tip-over standard mandatory since 2023: all three only began shipping more than a year after that deadline, not before it

Between 3 and 10 September 2026, CPSC recalled three more clothing storage unit lines, FJTJBSI's six-drawer chests, Gizoon Direct's six-drawer dressers and Alanca's six-drawer dressers sold via Wayfair, more than 13,600 units combined, for tip-over and entrapment hazards that violate 16 CFR Part 1261, the mandatory stability standard applicable to units manufactured after 1 September 2023. None is legacy stock caught mid-transition: all three began shipping in 2024 or 2025, well after the deadline. Clothing storage units are a children's product under the CPSA, which requires a lab-tested Children's Product Certificate before import, the same documentation gap three unrelated, China-based supply chains kept exposing three years into the rule.

What the three September recalls actually have in common

Each of the three recalls follows the template CPSC now applies to dozens of clothing storage unit cases a year: a six-drawer wood or fabric dresser, unstable enough to tip over if not anchored to a wall, sold through a single online marketplace, recalled for violating 16 CFR Part 1261 rather than because of any injury already reported. The standard, adopted from the voluntary ASTM F2057-23 specification, sets a stability test: a unit's tip-over moment, the force needed to make it fall forward, has to exceed a threshold moment calculated from its own weight and dimensions, tested with drawers open and a defined added load simulating a climbing child. The rule also requires a permanent warning label and, since 1 September 2023, bars the sale of any newly manufactured unit that fails the test. What distinguishes the September 2026 batch is timing, not the test result. FJTJBSI's dressers reached Amazon in November 2024, fourteen months after the compliance date. Alanca's reached Wayfair in April 2025, nineteen months after. Gizoon Direct's reached Amazon in May 2025, twenty months after. A unit manufactured that late has no argument that it predates the rule. Each brand should already have held a Children's Product Certificate, the CPSA Section 14 document certifying compliance on the basis of third-party lab test results, before the first unit ever reached a warehouse.

The real subject: a self-certification system with no check at listing

Most coverage of these recalls, when it appears at all, treats each one as a standalone consumer alert: stop using the dresser, anchor it to the wall, contact the company for a refund. That framing misses what three unrelated recalls landing in the same eight-day window actually show. Under Section 14 of the Consumer Product Safety Act, a clothing storage unit is a children's product subject to a CPSC safety rule, which means the manufacturer or importer must hold a Children's Product Certificate before the unit is imported or sold, built on test results from a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory rather than the manufacturer's own say-so. That certificate is meant to be the control that keeps a non-compliant dresser off the market in the first place. It manifestly did not work here: three brands, three separate factories, three different sales windows, all shipped units that failed the same physical test for between fourteen and twenty months before CPSC's own market surveillance, not an injury report, caught them. Neither Amazon nor Wayfair verifies a supplier's underlying lab report before a listing goes live; both rely on the seller's own certification. That leaves the certificate doing the job of a rubber stamp: it satisfies the paperwork requirement without anyone checking that the physical product behind it actually passed the test it claims to have passed. This is not a one-off cluster. Trade press tracking CPSC's recall log recorded three more clothing storage unit recalls in January 2026, two more in March 2026, and five recalls totalling nearly 15,000 units in a single week of February 2026, each citing the same 16 CFR Part 1261 violation. Three years into a rule built specifically to stop dressers from tipping over on children, the recurring failure point is not the test standard. It is the gap between the certificate a listing claims and the lab data that is supposed to sit behind it.

Why it matters for brands

Three groups should treat this pattern as an active control gap, not background noise. First, any brand or private-label seller sourcing dressers, wardrobes, bookshelves or other furniture meeting CPSC's clothing storage unit definition should treat a supplier's Children's Product Certificate as a starting point for verification, not proof of compliance: ask for the underlying third-party lab report, confirm the testing lab appears on CPSC's list of accepted laboratories, and check that the tested sample matches the exact dimensions and drawer configuration of the SKU actually shipping, since a certificate for a similar but not identical model does not cover the product being sold. Second, any brand selling through a marketplace rather than importing directly should not assume the platform has already run that check: Amazon and Wayfair both accept a seller's own certification at listing time, which is precisely the gap FJTJBSI, Gizoon Direct and Alanca each exploited, intentionally or not, for over a year before being caught. Third, any compliance team building or buying a product-data system for furniture or other children's products should treat 16 CFR Part 1261 the way these recalls suggest CPSC itself now treats it: as a recurring monthly enforcement target, not a 2023 deadline the industry has already cleared. A test report from three years ago, or from a different factory batch than the one currently shipping, is not evidence of ongoing compliance, and three unrelated companies just found that out in the same week.

Two ways to read the September recalls

The narrow read

Three more dresser brands got recalled in September for failing a stability test: owners should anchor the units to a wall or stop using them.

The structural read

Three unrelated, China-based supply chains shipped non-compliant units for fourteen to twenty months after a certificate was legally required before their first sale. The self-certification system meant to catch this before market entry is not catching it, and CPSC's own monthly recall cadence shows this case is typical, not exceptional.

Frequently asked questions

What safety standard did these three dresser recalls violate?
All three recalled dresser lines, FJTJBSI's six-drawer chests, Gizoon Direct's six-drawer double dressers and Alanca's six-drawer dressers, violate 16 CFR Part 1261, the federal stability standard the CPSC adopted from ASTM F2057-23 on 19 April 2023. The standard requires a clothing storage unit's tip-over moment to exceed a calculated threshold moment when tested with drawers open and a simulated child's weight applied, and it applies to any covered unit manufactured after 1 September 2023.
Does a Children's Product Certificate mean a dresser has actually been tested?
It is supposed to. Section 14 of the Consumer Product Safety Act requires a manufacturer or importer of a children's product subject to a CPSC safety rule, which includes clothing storage units, to issue a Children's Product Certificate based on test results from a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory before the product is imported or sold. In practice, neither CPSC nor the marketplaces where these three lines were sold verify the underlying lab report at the point of listing, which is why three separate, unrelated supply chains were able to sell non-compliant units for between fourteen and twenty months before being caught.
What should a brand check before listing a dresser or similar furniture item in the US?
Before listing any freestanding or stackable storage furniture that meets CPSC's clothing storage unit definition, a brand should obtain and review the actual third-party lab test report behind its supplier's Children's Product Certificate, confirm the testing lab appears on CPSC's list of accepted laboratories, and verify that the tested sample matches the exact dimensions, drawer configuration and weight of the SKU being shipped, since a certificate covering a similar model does not extend to a different one. Given that CPSC has recalled clothing storage units for this same violation in nearly every month of 2025 and 2026, a certificate issued once at product launch should not be treated as permanent proof of compliance for a SKU still in active production.

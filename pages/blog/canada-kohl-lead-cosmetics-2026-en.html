A recall Health Canada has, in effect, made before

Kohl, also sold as kajal or surma, is a dark powder traditionally applied to the eyes across Middle Eastern, South Asian and North African communities, for cosmetic, medicinal and religious reasons alike, including on infants. Some traditional recipes make it by grinding galena, a lead sulfide ore, into powder, which is precisely why it keeps failing heavy-metal testing decades after regulators first flagged the category. Health Canada's 4 September notice does not describe a new kind of hazard. It describes a hazard the department named by product category in 2005, tested and warned about again through subsequent decades, and is now finding, once more, in two specific branded products sold through a single Toronto-area retailer between January and August 2026. The recall itself is narrow: stop using the product, return it to Sara Islamic Store for a refund. What is not narrow is how long the underlying pattern, a product marketed as an herbal or traditional eye cosmetic that turns out to carry lead well past any impurity threshold, has been on Health Canada's own record.

Three details behind the 4 September recall

01

An "impurity" limit is the wrong yardstick for an intentional ingredient

Health Canada's 10 ppm guidance covers lead that enters a cosmetic unintentionally, through pigments or raw materials. Lead and its compounds are separately banned outright as an ingredient on the Cosmetic Ingredient Hotlist. A recipe built around galena is not a contamination problem a supplier can clean up.

02

The same brand name already failed a test, in another country, in 2019

Al-Asmad Alharmain Zam Zam & Rose Water was tested by public health officials in Oregon in 2019 at 59 percent lead by weight. The identical branded product was still entering the Canadian market under the same name in 2026.

03

One storefront, 1,152 units, eight months of sales

Both products moved through a single specialty retailer, the kind of low-volume, single-location import channel that most compliance and customs risk-screening is not built to prioritize.

The real subject: a product record that never crossed a border

Most coverage of a lead-in-cosmetics recall reads it as a quality-control failure: a supplier let an impurity through, a batch tested badly, the manufacturer needs tighter sourcing. That framing fits accidental contamination. It does not fit a kohl formulated from ground galena, where the lead is not an impurity that slipped past screening, it is the pigment the recipe calls for, which is exactly why Health Canada bans lead and its compounds outright as a cosmetic ingredient on its Cosmetic Ingredient Hotlist, a stricter rule than the 10 ppm impurity guidance the 4 September recall notice cites. Calling this an impurity violation is technically the language the recall used, but it understates what compliance would actually require: not better manufacturing, a different product. The second gap is where the same recall becomes a data problem rather than a chemistry problem. Al-Asmad Alharmain Zam Zam & Rose Water did not need to be discovered in 2026. It was discovered, by name, at 59 percent lead, by public health officials across the border in 2019. Nothing about that 2019 finding attached itself to the product when it later entered the Canadian market under the identical brand name; it took a fresh, unconnected Canadian testing effort, and 576 units already sold, to reach the same conclusion a different country's health department had already published seven years earlier. A hazard finding on a named product, in any market, is durable information. Whether it travels to the next market that product enters is a data and process question, not a chemistry one, and on this evidence the answer was no.

Why it matters for brands

The direct exposure sits with retailers, marketplaces and specialty grocers expanding their assortment into traditional, cultural or religious personal-care goods, a real growth category for multi-category platforms serving diaspora communities. Labeling a product 'herbal,' 'natural' or 'traditional' changes nothing about which regulatory regime applies: once an item is a cosmetic under the Food and Drugs Act, Canada's heavy-metal impurity guidance and its outright ban on lead as an ingredient apply exactly as they would to a mainstream eyeliner, and a recipe rooted in centuries of tradition is not a compliance exemption. The more durable lesson is about product history, not product chemistry. A brand name that has already failed a public health test in one country is a checkable fact before it is listed in another, not something a buyer discovers only after a local recall. Any retailer sourcing traditional or imported personal-care items, directly or through a network of small suppliers, gains more from screening incoming SKUs against prior international recalls and public health findings under the same product and brand name than from testing each shipment in isolation and hoping the last market's findings do not repeat. The channel this recall moved through, a single storefront selling under 600 units of each item over eight months, is itself a signal worth building into that screening: low-volume, single-location import routes are exactly where a previously flagged product is most likely to resurface unnoticed.

Two ways to read 4 September

The narrow read

Health Canada recalled two traditional kohl products sold by one small retailer after finding lead above its cosmetics guidance.

The structural read

One of the two recalled products, under the identical brand name, was already measured at 58,600 times the same lead threshold by a different country's health officials seven years earlier, and Health Canada has publicly warned about this exact product category since 2005: the gap this recall exposes is not a testing failure, it is the absence of any record that follows a named product across the borders and years it keeps crossing.

Frequently asked questions

What did Health Canada recall on 4 September 2026, and why?
Health Canada recalled two traditional kohl eye cosmetics, Mumtaz Herbal Kajal With Almond Oil for Sensitive Eyes and Al-Asmad Alharmain Zam Zam & Rose Water, both sold through Sara Islamic Store, after finding lead above the level set in its Guidance on Heavy Metal Impurities in Cosmetics, 10 parts per million. The company reported 576 units of each product sold in Canada between January and August 2026, and no reports of illness or injury as of 28 August 2026.
Why is lead in traditional kohl not just an accidental impurity?
Some traditional kohl recipes are made by grinding galena, a lead sulfide ore, into powder as the coloring agent itself, not as a contaminant that entered by accident. Health Canada's 10 ppm guidance is written for unavoidable trace impurities; lead and its compounds are separately and completely banned as an ingredient on the country's Cosmetic Ingredient Hotlist, a stricter rule that a recipe built around galena cannot meet through better sourcing or manufacturing alone.
Why did a product already flagged in the US in 2019 still enter Canada in 2026?
A hazard finding on a named product published by health officials in one country does not automatically attach to that product when it is later imported and sold under the identical brand name in another market. Health Canada's 4 September 2026 recall of Al-Asmad Alharmain Zam Zam & Rose Water reached, through its own independent testing, the same conclusion Oregon public health officials had already published in 2019, seven years and one border apart.

A safety standard, not just a stricter hygiene rule

GB 7916-1987 was a hygiene standard: it set baseline cleanliness and contamination limits for cosmetics sold in China, and had not been rewritten in the standard's nearly forty-year life. GB 7916-2026 replaces it with a foundational, mandatory safety standard covering general requirements, raw-material requirements, product requirements, packaging, labelling, storage and transport, and how the standard itself is to be implemented. SAMR and the NMPA describe it as upgrading the regulatory logic from hygiene administration to comprehensive safety governance, the same shift the EU made years earlier when its Cosmetic Products Regulation moved from a positive-list hygiene approach to a full safety-assessment framework. The standard sets limit values for eight restricted substances, with China's own regulator describing the resulting heavy-metal control as internationally leading, and existing stock produced or imported before 1 January 2028 can continue to be sold through the end of its shelf life, so the standard does not force an immediate recall of compliant inventory already in the market.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

China's children's cosmetics run to age 12, not age 3

Most international microbial regimes reserve their strictest children's limit for products aimed at children under 3. GB 7916-2026 applies its 100 CFU/g cap to any cosmetic formulated for children up to 12, a far wider product band than most global compliance systems are mapped against.

02

The test methods arrived three days before the standard

NMPA Announcement No. 72 of 2026, dated 3 August, released official test methods for diclofenac sodium and hexamine in cosmetics. Publishing the enforcement tools just ahead of the standard itself signals labs and regulators were meant to be ready to test against it immediately.

03

A long runway does not mean a quiet one

The standard does not bind the market until 1 January 2028, seventeen months out. But reformulation, retesting to the new eight-substance limits, and rebuilding age-banded product files for a 12-and-under category is exactly the kind of work that takes longer than brands expect once they start.

The numbers behind 6 August

One number is the microbial ceiling children's cosmetics now have to meet. One is how much stricter that is than the general-cosmetics limit. The third is how long brands have before the standard actually binds the market.

100 CFU/g

the total bacterial count limit GB 7916-2026 sets for cosmetics formulated for children up to 12 years old, per gram or millilitre of product

10x

how much stricter that children's limit is than the microbial ceiling GB 7916-2026 sets for general, non-children's cosmetics

17 months

the gap between GB 7916-2026's 6 August 2026 publication and its 1 January 2028 effective date, before the 1987 hygiene standard it replaces is abolished

The real subject: an age band, not just a bacteria count

A brand that already tracks children's cosmetics compliance against an under-3 threshold, the band most international microbial and formulation rules use, has a system built around the wrong cut-off for China. GB 7916-2026 does not just tighten a number; it widens who counts as a child for microbial-limit purposes, up to 12 years old, three times wider than the international norm. A bath product, a costume make-up kit or a skincare line marketed to a 6-year-old or a 10-year-old, products that many global compliance systems would file as general cosmetics because they sit outside the under-3 band, now falls inside China's strictest microbial category regardless of how it is classified anywhere else. Children's cosmetics sold into China also remain separately governed by China's dedicated children's cosmetics regulation, so GB 7916-2026 does not replace that regime, it adds a general safety floor underneath it. Getting this right is a classification exercise before it is a lab exercise: knowing which SKUs fall inside China's 12-and-under band comes first, testing against the 100 CFU/g limit comes second.

Why it matters for brands

For any cosmetics, personal-care or beauty brand selling into China, directly or through a local distributor or cross-border e-commerce channel, three things follow from 6 August. First, re-run the age classification on the catalogue: any SKU marketed to, or reasonably intended for, children up to 12, not just under 3, needs to be flagged for the stricter 100 CFU/g microbial limit, a wider net than most brands' existing children's-product tagging catches today. Second, treat the 1 January 2028 effective date as a formulation and testing deadline, not a labelling one: meeting the standard's eight-substance limit values and the new microbial ceiling for a widened children's category can require reformulation, and reformulation timelines routinely run longer than the seventeen months the standard nominally allows. Third, watch the enforcement infrastructure move in step with the standard: NMPA published binding test methods for two named substances just three days before the safety standard itself, evidence that China intends the underlying prohibited-substance checks to be enforceable from early in the transition period, not treated as aspirational until 2028. Brands that hold cosmetics safety and ingredient data as structured, per-SKU, per-market records can re-run this China-specific age classification as a query. Brands whose children's-product definition lives in a single global policy document have to reopen every children's and family-marketed SKU by hand to find out which ones the 12-and-under band newly reaches.

Two ways to read 6 August

The narrow read

China published a stricter mandatory cosmetics safety standard, replacing a nearly forty-year-old hygiene rule, effective 1 January 2028.

The structural read

China redrew who counts as a child for its strictest microbial limit, from the internationally common under-3 band to under-12, and released the test methods to enforce prohibited-substance limits three days before the standard itself: the compliance work is reclassifying which SKUs the wider band now reaches, not reading a new bacteria count off a spec sheet.

Frequently asked questions

What is GB 7916-2026 and when does it take effect?
GB 7916-2026, Cosmetics: General Safety Requirements, is China's first mandatory national safety standard for cosmetics, published by the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) on 6 August 2026. It replaces GB 7916-1987, the Hygienic Standard for Cosmetics that had governed the market for nearly forty years, and takes effect on 1 January 2028, when the 1987 standard is simultaneously abolished. Products already produced or imported before that date can continue to be sold through the end of their shelf life.
Which products count as "children's cosmetics" under the new microbial limit?
GB 7916-2026 applies its strictest total bacterial count limit, no more than 100 CFU per gram or millilitre, ten times stricter than the general-cosmetics limit, to any cosmetic formulated for children up to 12 years old. That is a wider band than most international microbial standards use, which typically reserve this strict a limit for products aimed at children under 3. A brand's existing under-3 children's-product classification will not by itself identify every SKU this rule now reaches in China.
What should cosmetics brands selling into China do before 1 January 2028?
Brands should re-run age classification across their catalogue to flag any SKU marketed to, or reasonably intended for, children up to 12, not just under 3, for the stricter 100 CFU/g microbial limit, and confirm formulations meet the standard's limit values for eight restricted substances. Because NMPA released binding test methods for named substances, diclofenac sodium and hexamine, just three days before the standard itself, brands should also expect enforcement infrastructure to be ready early in the transition period rather than only from 2028.

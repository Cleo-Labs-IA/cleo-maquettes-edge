A toy fad meets a five-month-old rule

Squishy toys, soft, slow-rebounding novelty toys often filled with foam or expanding water beads, have been one of the fastest-moving categories on marketplaces and in dollar-store aisles for the past two years, exactly the kind of category counterfeiters copy fastest: cheap to mold, easy to source in bulk, sold mostly on price and packaging rather than brand recognition. CPSC's alert describes fake and counterfeit versions as failing federal safety requirements covering water beads, small parts, lead and phthalates, the same substantive limits that apply to any toy sold to children 12 and under: no more than 100 ppm of total lead in an accessible component, no more than 0.1% of eight regulated phthalates in an accessible plasticized part. Those limits are more than fifteen years old, dating to the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act. What is not fifteen years old is the water-bead-specific standard: CPSC proposed it in September 2024, finalized it in the Federal Register on 12 December 2025 as 16 CFR 1250, and it took effect on 12 March 2026, less than five months before this alert. A genuine squishy toy sold in the U.S. today has to pass a size-limit test, the fully expanded bead must pass through a 5.0 mm gauge, stay under 325 micrograms of extractable acrylamide per 100 small beads or per single large bead, and carry a strongly worded label stating that children have died and others have required surgery. None of that is a requirement a counterfeiter has any reason to meet, and CPSC's own hazard description, ingestion, choking, aspiration, insertion into the nose or ear, is precisely what the new rule was written to reduce.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

The enforcement numbers sat under the safety tips

Nearly every outlet that covered the alert repeated the same parenting checklist: don't microwave it, watch for odor. Fewer reported that CPSC had already identified 55 shipments and kept 355,683 units out of the country, or that it issued notices of violation to named importers.

02

The bar for genuine toys rose five months ago

16 CFR 1250 took effect on 12 March 2026, adding a bead-expansion gauge test, an acrylamide ceiling and a new mandatory warning label. Most of squishy toys' viral run happened before that standard existed.

03

The action names importers, not just a product type

CPSC's alert states it issued notices of violation to the specific companies responsible for bringing the noncompliant units in or selling them, not just a generic warning about squishy toys as a category.

The numbers behind 5 August

One number is the scale of what CPSC says it kept out of the country. Two are technical: the decade-old substance limits every children's toy must meet, and the newer physical and chemical limits a water-bead toy specifically has had to meet for less than five months.

The real subject: a compliant toy and a counterfeit one look the same, the difference is a data trail

A squishy toy is defined by touch and rebound speed, not by a feature a customs officer or a shopper can check at a glance, so nothing about a fake one necessarily looks different from a compliant one on a shelf or in a listing photo. What is different is a stack of records that never travels with the physical object itself. Under the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act, every children's toy imported into the U.S. already needs a Children's Product Certificate backed by third-party lab testing before it can legally enter the country, covering the lead and phthalate limits that predate this alert by more than a decade. Since 12 March 2026, a squishy toy containing water beads needs a second, newer file behind it too: a test report against 16 CFR 1250's expansion-gauge and acrylamide limits, plus warning-label copy that has to match wording CPSC specified rather than whatever a factory already had on hand. CPSC's 5 August alert reports 55 shipments identified as violative and 355,683 units kept out, a scale that points to a documentation and targeting exercise rather than a warehouse of technicians unit-testing every squishy toy that arrives. Whatever the precise screening method, the substance being checked is the same for a brand's own compliant SKU: not whether the toy looks right, but whether the certification and test-record file behind that specific shipment is complete and current.

Why it matters for brands

For any retail or consumer-goods brand selling squishy toys, or any other toy containing water beads, into the United States, three things follow from 5 August. First, a Children's Product Certificate issued before 12 March 2026 no longer covers a water-bead-containing product on its own: 16 CFR 1250 is a separate, additive standard with its own expansion-gauge and acrylamide tests and its own required warning-label wording, and a brand relying on pre-2026 paperwork for that category has a gap regardless of how compliant its lead and phthalate testing already is. Second, counterfeiters copying a viral, fast-moving product undercut price precisely by skipping this file altogether, and CPSC's own enforcement numbers, 55 shipments and 355,683 units in one alert, show the agency is actively screening for that exact gap at the border rather than waiting for a complaint to arrive. Third, notices of violation named specific importers and sellers, not anonymous SKUs: a compliant brand whose own documentation is incomplete, inconsistent across SKUs, or slow to update after a rule change like 16 CFR 1250 risks being read by the same screening as indistinguishable from an actual counterfeit, since the check runs on the paperwork trail, not on how the toy looks in a listing photo.

Two ways to read 5 August

The narrow read

CPSC warned parents about fake squishy toys, and said it kept 355,683 counterfeit units out of the United States across 55 identified shipments.

The structural read

The alert landed five months into a new, more detailed federal standard for water-bead toys, so the compliance file that separates a genuine squishy toy from a knockoff just grew, and enforcement is already testing that gap at the border before most brands have updated their own paperwork to match.

Frequently asked questions

What did CPSC announce in its 5 August 2026 alert about counterfeit squishy toys?
CPSC issued a Consumer Safety Alert warning that fake and counterfeit squishy toys fail to meet federal safety requirements covering water beads, small parts, lead and phthalates, and it reiterated an earlier warning about children being burned by a viral trend of microwaving the toys. CPSC said it had identified 55 shipments of violative squishy toys at U.S. ports and worked with Customs and Border Protection to keep 355,683 units out of the country, and that it issued notices of violation to the companies responsible for importing or selling them.
What is 16 CFR 1250 and when did it take effect?
16 CFR 1250 is CPSC's first mandatory federal safety standard specifically for water bead toys and toys containing water beads. CPSC proposed it in September 2024, finalized it in the Federal Register on 12 December 2025, and it took effect on 12 March 2026. It requires expanded beads to pass through a 5.0 mm test gauge, caps extractable acrylamide at 325 micrograms per 100 small beads or one large bead, and mandates a strongly worded warning label stating that children have died and others have required surgery.
What should brands selling squishy or water-bead toys into the U.S. take away from the 5 August alert?
A Children's Product Certificate issued before 12 March 2026 does not on its own cover the new water-bead-specific requirements in 16 CFR 1250, so brands need a separate, current test record for expansion size, acrylamide content and warning-label wording alongside their existing lead and phthalate testing. Because CPSC's enforcement runs on that documentation trail rather than on how a toy looks, a genuinely compliant brand with incomplete or inconsistent per-SKU records risks being screened the same way as an actual counterfeit.

What actually changed, and what happened in June

Indonesia's Law No. 33 of 2014 on Halal Product Assurance first made halal certification compulsory, and Government Regulation No. 42 of 2024 set the current phased timeline for enforcing it: food and beverages became mandatory from 17 October 2024, with a relaxation period for micro and small enterprises; cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, traditional medicines, health supplements and certain other consumer goods become mandatory from 17 October 2026, the same date the food and beverage relaxation period ends. BPJPH (the Halal Product Assurance Organizing Agency, under the Ministry of Religious Affairs) is the body that certifies products, accredits halal auditors, and now enforces the regime. Two developments in June 2026 turned that timeline from a date on a calendar into an active enforcement framework. On 5 June, BPJPH Regulation No. 2 of 2026 on the Imposition of Administrative Sanctions for Violations of Halal Product Assurance Implementation came into force, setting out an escalating sanctions ladder that reaches business actors, halal auditors and certification-chain institutions alike. The day before, on 4 June, Indonesia notified the WTO (G/TBT/N/IDN/187) of a draft BPJPH decree laying out guidelines for the Halal Assurance System (Sistem Jaminan Produk Halal, SJPH) specific to cosmetics, identifying the critical control points a manufacturer has to manage across the ingredient and production chain. That draft is open for comment until 2 August 2026, meaning the technical definition of "compliant" for cosmetics is still being finalized barely eleven weeks before the deadline it is meant to enforce.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

The deadline is old; the rulebook is new

GR 42/2024 fixed 17 October 2026 as the cosmetics deadline. The technical guidance defining a compliant ingredient chain is still a draft, open for comment until 2 August 2026.

02

Enforcement got teeth in June, not October

BPJPH Regulation No. 2 of 2026 built the sanctions ladder (warning, fine, certificate revocation, recall, premises closure, public disclosure) and put it into force from 5 June, months ahead of the deadline itself.

03

It tests ingredient segregation, not the formula

The draft cosmetics control points center on keeping halal and non-halal ingredients from mixing at receipt, storage and production: proof of origin and separation, not reformulation.

The numbers behind the deadline

Three dates carry the substance of this story. One is the deadline everyone already has on a calendar. The other two are the dates the enforcement and technical-compliance machinery actually came online: both in the same first week of June 2026, both easy to miss if a brand is only tracking 17 October.

The real subject: ingredient-origin data, not a certificate to file away

Framed as a certification requirement, 17 October 2026 sounds like paperwork: apply, get a certificate, done. The draft SJPH guidelines for cosmetics describe something more granular, a set of critical control points that a manufacturer has to manage across the ingredient and production chain, starting with ingredient receipt: confirming that halal and non-halal materials are never mixed, and that storage facilities keep them physically separated, before a control point structure that extends through production. That is a supply-chain segregation and provenance requirement, not a finished-product label claim. It means knowing, ingredient by ingredient, whether a ubiquitous cosmetics input (glycerin, collagen, gelatin, certain emulsifiers, the alcohol used in a formulation) is of plant, synthetic or animal origin, because that origin, not the finished product's appearance or function, is what a halal control point is checking. Most global brands' compliance data is built around what an ingredient does (INCI declarations, safety data, concentration limits); the SJPH framework asks a different question about the same ingredient list: where it came from, and whether it ever touched a non-segregated line. A separate WTO notification, filed in November 2025, defines the scope of all this by HS code rather than by product description, meaning correct tariff classification determines up front whether a given imported SKU is even inside the regime.

Why it matters for brands

This reaches any brand manufacturing or importing cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, traditional medicines, health supplements or certain other consumer goods for the Indonesian market: the world's largest Muslim-majority market by population, and one where BPJPH has already shown, through market-withdrawal action on food and beverage since its 2024 phase, that it enforces rather than merely publishes deadlines. Three separate workstreams converge on the same date. First, an HS-code question: the WTO-notified tariff list determines whether a specific SKU is in scope at all, which means product classification has to be checked before a certification strategy is even built. Second, an ingredient-origin question: a brand's existing INCI-based ingredient documentation almost certainly does not capture the plant/synthetic/animal-origin distinction the SJPH control points require, so building that data set now, rather than in September, is the only way to avoid a scramble against the 2 August comment deadline and the 17 October enforcement date landing eleven weeks apart. Third, a sanctions question: BPJPH Regulation No. 2 of 2026 gives regulators a working escalation path (warning, fine, certificate revocation, product recall, premises closure, public disclosure) that did not exist in this form before June, and it applies to the whole certification chain, not just the brand placing the product on the market.

Two ways to read 17 October

The narrow read

A certification deadline set back in 2024 arrives on schedule for cosmetics and a handful of other categories. Apply for the certificate before October.

The structural read

The sanctions regime enforcing the deadline only came into force in June, and the technical rules defining a compliant ingredient chain for cosmetics are still open for comment, so brands are being asked to assemble plant/synthetic/animal-origin data for ingredients most compliance systems have never tracked, against a rulebook that finishes just eleven weeks before enforcement starts.

Frequently asked questions

When does Indonesia require halal certification for cosmetics?
From 17 October 2026, under Government Regulation No. 42 of 2024 implementing Indonesia's 2014 Halal Product Assurance Law. Mandatory halal certification, already in force for food and beverages since 17 October 2024, extends on that date to cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, traditional medicines, health supplements and certain other consumer goods, and the relaxation period that had exempted micro and small food and beverage enterprises also ends.
What happens to non-compliant cosmetics sold in Indonesia after the deadline?
BPJPH Regulation No. 2 of 2026, in force since 5 June 2026, sets an escalating administrative-sanctions ladder for violations of halal product assurance requirements: a written warning, an administrative fine, revocation of the halal certificate or foreign certificate registration, product recall or withdrawal from circulation, temporary closure of business premises, and public disclosure. The sanctions regime reaches business actors as well as halal auditors and other institutions in the certification chain, not just the brand placing the product on the market.
What are the "critical control points" in Indonesia's draft halal cosmetics guidelines?
Indonesia notified the WTO on 4 June 2026 (G/TBT/N/IDN/187) of draft BPJPH guidelines for the Halal Assurance System (SJPH) specific to cosmetics, open for public comment until 2 August 2026. The guidelines identify critical control points across the ingredient and production chain (starting with ingredient receipt, where halal and non-halal materials must not be mixed and storage facilities must keep them physically separated) requiring manufacturers to document ingredient origin (plant, synthetic or animal) and prove segregation, not just declare a finished-product claim.

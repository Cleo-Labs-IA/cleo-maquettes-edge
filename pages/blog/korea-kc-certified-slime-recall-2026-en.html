On 16 September, Korea's national standards agency recalled two KC-certified slime toys for a banned preservative: the seven other failures it found, all labelled 'for ages 14 and up,' got a shelf-separation order instead of a recall

On 16 September 2026, Korea's KATS published results from testing 76 tactile toys: 37 KC-certified children's products and 39 labelled 'for ages 14 and up.' Nine failed. Two certified slime products, Pinkfoot Slime and Honey Slime, contained banned preservatives and excess boron and were recalled and blocked from roughly 290,000 stores. Seven age-exempt products failed worse, phthalates at 1.3 to 3.4 times the limit and boron up to 11.4 times it, but got only a shelf-separation directive, since a '14 and up' label keeps a product outside Korea's certification and recall regime entirely, the same gap Seoul's city government had flagged in a separate test two and a half weeks earlier.

Two failure groups, two different legal levers

KATS ran its 76 tactile toys through the physical and chemical tests set for children's products, splitting the sample by legal status rather than by product type: 37 items already carried the mandatory KC mark, meaning a manufacturer had certified them, on the basis of accredited lab testing, as compliant children's products; 39 carried a printed usage age of '14 and older,' which under Korea's Special Act on the Safety of Children's Products removes them from that certification requirement entirely, since the Act defines a children's product by the age printed on the box rather than by the toy's actual design, marketing or buyer. Among the 37 certified items, two slime products failed for banned preservatives, methylisothiazolinone and chloromethylisothiazolinone, chemicals barred outright from children's products, plus boron readings of 4.7 and 7.2 times the legal limit. Because both carried the KC mark, KATS had clear authority to act: it ordered both recalled and registered them in the Hazardous Product Sales Blocking System, a database linked to roughly 290,000 retail outlets nationwide that stops registered items from being resold. Among the 39 age-exempt items, seven failed the same test battery, worse on several measures: phthalate plasticizers at 1.3 to 3.4 times the limit, boron at up to 11.4 times it, and banned preservatives in some cases. None of the seven qualified for a recall order, because none had ever been inside the certification perimeter that gives KATS its recall authority in the first place.

Three details behind the 16 September findings

01

KC certification did not catch two failures

Two of the 37 KC-certified children's slime products, already vetted as compliant, still reached shelves nationwide carrying a preservative banned outright from children's products.

02

The 14-and-up label routes around KC entirely

The same mechanism Seoul flagged two and a half weeks earlier: none of the 39 adult-labelled products needed to pass KC testing, whatever the toy's marketing or actual young buyers.

03

KATS could order a nationwide recall and sales block for the two certified failures, but only a shelf-separation directive for the seven uncertified failures, which scored worse on chemical limits.

The real subject: two independent gaps hiding inside one number

Most coverage of this review, where it appears at all, reads as a single consumer alert: nine toxic squishy toys found, check the label before buying. That framing collapses two structurally different failures into one headline number. The first is a verification gap inside the certification system itself: KC certification is supposed to mean a manufacturer has produced accredited lab evidence that a specific product meets children's safety standards, yet two of the 37 certified slime products in this review still reached roughly 290,000 stores carrying a preservative barred outright from children's use, not merely a level slightly over a limit, a banned substance. That gap only surfaced because KATS tested finished retail units rather than relying on the certificate on file. The second is a classification gap that Seoul had already flagged in city-level testing on 31 August: a printed '14 and up' label removes a product from the certification and recall regime entirely, regardless of the toy's cute-mascot marketing or who is actually observed buying it, and KATS's own national numbers now show that gap producing a worse failure rate, 17.9% against 5.4%, than the certification system it sits outside of. Both gaps were visible in the same 76-product sample, and only one of them, the one triggered by a certificate on file, gives KATS the legal authority to order a recall. KATS says it plans to tighten the technical standards for tactile-toy materials and surface chemicals; nothing in the 16 September announcement extends mandatory certification, or recall authority, to the 14-and-up bracket itself.

Why it matters for brands

Three groups should treat this as an active control gap rather than a one-off toy scare. First, any brand selling a KC-certified children's product into Korea should treat the certificate as a starting point, not an ongoing guarantee: this review shows the design-stage lab report behind a KC mark did not stop a banned preservative from reaching a later production run, so periodic batch-level retesting, not a certificate issued once at launch, is what actually catches a formulation change made by a supplier to cut cost. Second, any brand using a '14 and up' or similar age label to keep a product outside a stricter children's regime, in Korea or elsewhere, should test that decision against the product's real design and marketing, not just the printed number: Korean regulators have now targeted that exact bracket twice in seventeen days, first at city level and then nationally, and a brand whose only compliance argument is the label on the box should expect to be the next one tested, with no certification and no recall infrastructure in place if it fails. Third, for any team building or buying product-data or PLM systems that cover the Korean market, 'KC certified: yes' is not, on its own, a sufficient compliance signal, and neither is 'age-exempt: yes': this case shows both fields can mislead independently, and a data model needs to track certificate status, printed target age and current lab data separately, and flag any SKU where a mismatch between marketing and printed age puts it in the same exposed bracket these nine failures came from.

Two ways to read the 16 September results

The narrow read

KATS found harmful chemicals in nine squishy and slime toys, two certified and seven not, recalled the two, and told stores to separate the rest from children's shelves.

The structural read

Korea's tactile-toy safety regime has two independent gaps running at once: a verification gap inside KC certification itself, and a classification gap that a single printed age still fully exploits three weeks after a city government flagged the identical loophole. Neither gap has closed, and KATS's own figures now show the classification gap producing a higher failure rate than the certification system it sits entirely outside of.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly did KATS find in its 16 September 2026 tactile toy review?
KATS, part of Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy, tested 76 tactile and slime toys sold under names including Mallang-i and Wakppu, 37 carrying mandatory KC children's-product certification and 39 labelled 'for ages 14 and up' and therefore exempt from it. Nine failed: two KC-certified slime products, Pinkfoot Slime and Honey Slime, contained banned preservatives (MIT and CMIT) and excess boron and were recalled and blocked from sale nationwide; seven age-exempt products exceeded limits for phthalate plasticizers and boron, or contained banned preservatives, but received a shelf-separation directive rather than a recall.
Does KC certification guarantee a Korean children's toy is free of banned chemicals?
Not reliably, on this evidence. KC certification is meant to attest, on the basis of accredited third-party lab testing, that a specific children's product meets Korea's safety standards. In this review, two of the 37 KC-certified slime products still reached roughly 290,000 stores carrying preservatives banned outright from children's products, a gap that only surfaced because KATS tested finished retail units directly rather than relying on the certificate on file. A KC mark reflects the test result behind the certificate at the time it was issued, not a continuing guarantee that every later production run matches it.
Why weren't the seven "14 and up" labelled toys recalled if they failed the same safety test?
Because Korea's Special Act on the Safety of Children's Products, and the KC certification and recall regime it creates, is triggered by the age printed on a product's packaging rather than by its chemistry or its actual buyers. A '14 and up' label keeps a product outside that regime entirely, so even when KATS tests such a product against children's safety standards and it fails, the agency has no recall authority to invoke, since that authority is tied to the certification perimeter the product was never inside. KATS's only available response was a shelf-separation directive to retailers, the same limitation the Seoul Metropolitan Government hit when it tested comparable products on 31 August 2026.

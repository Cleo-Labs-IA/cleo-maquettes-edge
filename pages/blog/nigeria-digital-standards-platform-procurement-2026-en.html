What actually changed, and how the two gates stack

The Digital Standards Platform is a joint BPP-SON system meant to give procurement officers, contractors and suppliers digital access to national standards, certification services and verified information on locally manufactured products. BPP Director-General Dr Adebowale A. Adedokun framed it as an economic-policy instrument at the launch: "Public procurement is far more than paperwork. It is the single most powerful instrument of economic policy in the hands of government… At the end of the day, it is about one Nigeria, one standard, one patronage and one trust." SON Director-General Dr Ifeanyi Okeke described it as marking "a new era in Nigeria's standards administration by digitising access to standards and conformity assessment services." Officials said that once the DSP is fully integrated into the BPP e-Marketplace, procurement officers will be able to determine which standard applies to a given procurement at the planning stage, before a tender is even published, while suppliers get clear product-specification guidance ahead of bidding. That integration timeline was not given a fixed date at launch. What was announced as effective immediately is narrower but harder-edged: the NIS Attestation Certificate obtained via the DSP is now a mandatory bid document and evaluation requirement for federal procurement wherever an applicable Nigerian Industrial Standard exists.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

The certificate gates the bid, not just the contract

The NIS Attestation Certificate is checked at bid evaluation, before award: it has to be matched against the applicable standard for the specific goods, works or service category being tendered.

02

It stacks on a separate local-sourcing test

Since May 2025, the "Nigeria First" policy already requires a BPP waiver before an MDA can procure a foreign good or service available locally. The DSP test runs alongside it, not instead of it.

03

The automotive rollout shows how granular this gets

Since 22 May 2026, BPP and NADDC require vehicle procurement to be checked against a register of NADDC-approved local assemblers, down to Vehicle Identification Number verification.

May 2025

Nigeria's Federal Executive Council approves the "Nigeria First" policy, prioritising Nigerian-made goods and services in federal procurement; an Executive Order gives it legal force days later, requiring a BPP waiver before buying a foreign alternative available locally.

22 May 2026

BPP and the National Automotive Design and Development Council (NADDC) sign the Nigeria First implementation framework for the automotive sector, requiring MDAs to source vehicles from NADDC-approved local assemblers, checked down to Vehicle Identification Number.

17 Jul 2026

The real subject: applicable-standard matching becomes a bid-eligibility data check

Framed as a quality-certification launch, the DSP sounds like a new form added to an existing bid pack. What the mechanism actually does is move a judgment call (which Nigerian Industrial Standard applies to this particular good, work or service) out of a procurement officer's discretion and into a checkable digital record that has to exist before a bid clears evaluation. That is a classification question about the product, not about the bidder's general reputation or price. It sits next to, not inside, the "Nigeria First" test, which asks a different classification question about the same shipment: is this good already available from a local source, such that a foreign supplier needs a BPP waiver to bid at all. A brand's product now has to answer two separate data questions (is it locally available, and does it hold a matching standards attestation) before a tender is even opened, and the automotive sector's NADDC framework, which checks compliance down to individual Vehicle Identification Numbers, is the clearest signal yet of how granular Nigeria intends the second question to get once the DSP is fully wired into the e-Marketplace.

Why it matters for brands

This reaches any brand that supplies physical goods, equipment or materials into Nigerian federal procurement (vehicles, medical devices, safety equipment, construction materials, electronics, school and hospital supplies) whether bidding directly or through a local distributor. First, a sourcing check: before a foreign-made SKU can even be tendered, a brand has to establish whether an equivalent is already available from a local source under the Nigeria First policy; if it is, bidding requires a BPP waiver, a separate administrative step that has nothing to do with product quality. Second, a standards-matching check: the specific Nigerian Industrial Standard applicable to that product category has to be identified correctly and matched to a valid NIS Attestation Certificate obtained through the DSP, a generic quality certificate from another market is not a substitute, and getting the applicable-standard classification wrong risks the bid being excluded at evaluation rather than merely queried. Third, a granularity check: the automotive sector's rollout, which verifies compliance down to individual Vehicle Identification Numbers against a register of approved local assemblers, is the template Nigeria has already built for enforcing this at unit level, brands in other regulated categories should expect the same precision once the DSP's e-Marketplace integration is complete, not treat today's certificate requirement as the ceiling.

Frequently asked questions

What is Nigeria's Digital Standards Platform and what does it require?
The Digital Standards Platform (DSP) is a joint system launched by Nigeria's Bureau of Public Procurement (BPP) and Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) on 17 July 2026 in Abuja, built with technical partner Goringo Consult Limited. From launch, a Nigerian Industrial Standards (NIS) Attestation Certificate obtained through the DSP is a mandatory bid document and evaluation requirement for federal procurement of any goods, works or services covered by an applicable Nigerian Industrial Standard. Officials said that once the DSP is fully integrated into the BPP e-Marketplace, procurement officers will be able to determine the applicable standard at the planning stage, before a tender is published.
How does the NIS Attestation Certificate interact with Nigeria's 'Nigeria First' local-sourcing policy?
They are two separate, stacked classification tests on the same shipment. Since May 2025, the 'Nigeria First' policy has required government ministries, departments and agencies (MDAs) to obtain a Bureau of Public Procurement waiver before procuring a foreign good or service that is already available locally. The NIS Attestation Certificate is a separate, additional requirement introduced 17 July 2026: it verifies that a specific product matches its applicable Nigerian Industrial Standard, independent of whether the good is sourced locally or abroad. A supplier's product has to clear both tests, not one or the other.
How granular does Nigeria expect this compliance data to get?
The clearest precedent is the automotive sector. On 22 May 2026, BPP and the National Automotive Design and Development Council (NADDC) signed an implementation framework requiring MDAs to source vehicles from NADDC-approved local assemblers, with compliance checked down to individual Vehicle Identification Numbers against a register of approved assemblers. Officials have not set a fixed date for extending equivalent unit-level checks to other product categories via the DSP's planned e-Marketplace integration, but the automotive framework signals the level of precision Nigeria is building toward.

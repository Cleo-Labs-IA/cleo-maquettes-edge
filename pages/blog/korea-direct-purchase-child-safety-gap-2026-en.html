A named list, not a blanket certification gate

The current rules trace back to 16 May 2024, when Korea's government announced it would block direct-purchase imports outright for products in 80 named categories lacking KC certification: 34 children's-product categories including strollers and toys, 34 electrical and household categories including electric heating mats, and 12 household chemical categories including humidifier disinfectants. Within days, facing public backlash over fears the policy amounted to a de facto ban on ordinary overseas shopping, the government withdrew the blanket blocking mechanism on 20 May 2024. What survived is lighter: a public hazard registry through the Consumer24 portal, and periodic sample testing by NITS and, separately, city consumer-protection authorities, with clearance-block requests to Korea Customs Service issued case by case against specific noncompliant items once they are caught. Categories named on the original 80-item list still face an automatic KC requirement. Everything else enters on an honor system unless a testing round happens to sample it.

Three nuances that separate signal from noise

01

The worst category is the one not on the list

Water-play equipment is not among the 34 named children's-product categories that trigger an automatic KC-certification requirement for direct-purchase imports, unlike strollers and toys. It was still the single worst-performing category NITS tested, at 90 to 100% failure.

02

A blanket rule was proposed, then withdrawn in days

The May 2024 policy would have blocked all 80 named categories automatically. Public pressure over fears of a broad shopping ban forced a walk-back within four days, leaving today's narrower, sample-based enforcement in its place.

03

The gap is measured, not assumed

NITS's own August sweep put a number on the difference: 19% of direct-purchase products failed against 5% of domestically distributed products sampled in the first half of 2026, roughly a fourfold gap between the certified retail channel and the personal-import one.

The numbers behind 6 August

One number is the overall gap between the certified retail channel and the direct-purchase one. One is how concentrated the failures were inside a single product category. The third is how many named categories carry an automatic certification requirement at all, out of the far larger number a brand actually sells.

The real subject: an enumerated list has a blind spot where its authors did not look

A regime built on naming specific categories, strollers, toys, electric heating mats, works only as well as the list anticipates what needs naming. Korea's 34-category children's-product list was drafted in 2024 under public pressure to be narrow enough not to read as a shopping ban, and it reads today the way any fixed list drafted under a deadline reads two years later: comprehensive for the categories regulators had already been worrying about, silent on the ones they had not. Inflatable water-play equipment sits in that silence. It is unambiguously a children's product, sold to the same buyers, through the same platforms, for the same summer season as the toys and strollers the list does name, and nothing in Korea's direct-purchase rules stopped a single unit of it from clearing customs before NITS happened to test a batch in August. The 19% overall failure rate is the headline. The fact that one untested-by-default category returned 90 to 100% failure the first time anyone checked is the actual finding: a named-category enforcement model does not get safer as new product types appear, it gets a growing blind spot, until a testing round or an incident forces an update to the list.

Why it matters for brands

For any retail or consumer-goods brand selling children's, household or electrical products into Korea, directly or watching a category where personal imports compete for the same shelf, three things follow from 6 August. First, do not equate "my product needs KC certification" with "my product's category appears on Korea's 34-item children's list": the list is a narrow, named enumeration, not a description of every product type that is actually regulated, and a brand's own compliant SKU can sit in a category the list omits without that meaning the category is unregulated or safe, only that it is unscreened at the border by default. Second, expect the enforcement model itself to keep expanding case by case rather than through a single rule change: after the 2024 walk-back left Korea without a blanket blocking mechanism, NITS's tool is testing rounds that name specific noncompliant products and route them to Customs for a clearance block, which means any category that returns a bad result, water-play equipment now, whichever category is tested next, can move from unscreened to targeted within one published sweep. Third, treat the 19% versus 5% gap as a quantified argument, not a footnote: for a brand that holds KC certification and prices it into a product, Korea's own regulator just measured what that certification is worth against the direct-purchase alternative undercutting it, and structured, per-category compliance records are what let a brand make that case product by product rather than waiting for the next sweep to make it for them.

Frequently asked questions

What did Korea's National Institute of Technology and Standards find in its August 2026 direct-purchase sweep?
NITS tested 484 products bought through overseas direct-purchase platforms such as AliExpress, Temu and Amazon: 228 children's products, 111 household goods and 145 electrical products. Ninety-four items, 19% of the sample, failed Korean safety standards, close to four times the 5% non-compliance rate NITS recorded for domestically distributed products in the first half of 2026. The worst results came from inflatable water-play equipment: all 18 adult products tested failed, along with 18 of 20 children's versions, mostly for tube walls thinner than Korea's minimum thickness standard or a missing backup air chamber. NITS registered the 94 items on its public hazard database and asked Korea Customs Service to block their clearance.
Why do children's water-play products fail Korea's safety standards at such a high rate?
Korea already requires KC safety certification before a direct-purchase import is allowed in for 34 named children's-product categories, including strollers and toys, a list drafted in 2024. Water-play equipment such as swim tubes is not one of the named categories, so it enters on an honor system unless a testing round happens to sample it. That gap traces to 16 May 2024, when Korea's government announced it would block direct-purchase imports outright across 80 named categories, then withdrew the blanket blocking mechanism four days later after public backlash over fears it amounted to a broad shopping ban, leaving today's narrower, sample-based enforcement in its place.
What should brands take away from Korea's KC-certification gap for direct-purchase imports?
First, a product's absence from Korea's 34-item children's list does not mean the category is safe or unregulated, only that it is unscreened by default at the border. Second, enforcement expands case by case through testing rounds rather than through a single rule change, so any category that returns a bad result, like water-play equipment now, can move from unscreened to targeted within one published sweep. Third, the 19% versus 5% gap is a quantified argument for KC-certified brands: structured, per-category compliance records let a brand show why its certified product is worth the premium over the direct-purchase alternative Korea's own regulator just flagged.
